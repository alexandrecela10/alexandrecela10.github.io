Night’s Watch

Warns a lender when a company it lends to may not have enough usable cash for its next interest payment. Then it ranks which companies the lender's staff should handle first, by money at stake.

Lending to private companiesLoan monitoringProduct assignment, synthetic data

Problem

A lender can learn a borrower is short of cash only on the payment date, when the easy fixes are gone. The cash figure it watches includes money stuck in currencies that can't be converted or sent abroad. In the sample bank file, a company held $9.31M in cash, but only $796K was in currencies it could use to pay.

For

PlayerA fund that lends directly to private companies and collects interest in cash on a fixed schedule. Its borrowers hold cash in several currencies.
UserA credit analyst (a lender's employee who watches loans). They decide which flagged borrower to handle first today, and what to do.
BuyerThe head of credit. They decide how many false alarms to accept so no missed payment arrives without a warning, and whether the team adopts the tool.

Terms on this page

  • Cash interest payment: interest the borrower must pay in money on a set date.
  • PIK (payment in kind): interest added to the loan instead of paid.
  • Usable vs trapped cash: cash the borrower can convert and send / cash stuck behind currency rules.
  • Coverage: expected usable cash on the payment date divided by the payment due. Below 1 means short.
  • P10, P40, P50: low, lower-middle and middle guesses of usable cash on the payment date.
  • Principal: the amount lent, before interest.
  • Recoverability: how likely the lender gets its money back.
  • Recall / precision: share of missed payments the tool warned about / share of warnings that were right.

User stories

  1. Rank. As a credit analyst, when new bank balances arrive, I want borrowers listed by their risk of missing the next interest payment, worst first. Then I handle the most money I can still save first.
  2. Check. As a credit analyst, when a borrower is flagged, I want every number traced back to the original bank and loan record. Then I can check it before I call the borrower.
  3. Close the loop. As head of credit, I want each warning to end in a logged action. Then I can see no flagged loan was ignored, and the records help set the warning threshold.

Try it

Built as a product assignment for Liquidity, a private credit lender. The prototype and the deck below are the original work.

  1. Open the prototype: the portfolio is ranked worst first, by cash coverage and money at risk.
  2. Click a company to see its cash story: usable versus stuck cash, the trend, and why it is flagged. Each number traces to its source file.
  3. Log the action in the same page. Then read the deck for the full reasoning: signal, workflow, data roadmap and success measures.

All data is synthetic. No AI model call.

Details

Value equation

value = (outcome x likelihood) / (time delay x effort & sacrifice)

CREDIT ANALYST

Outcome
"I open the list and the borrower most likely to miss its interest payment, with the most money at stake, is on top. I can show why in one click."
Likelihood
MEDIUM. Every number is traced. The forecast assumes cash falls by the same amount each day. It hasn't been tested against real missed payments. No analyst has used it.
Delay
LOW. The demo recalculates every borrower whenever bank data changes. Time from real bank data to warning is unobserved: no live connection yet.
Effort
MEDIUM. No spreadsheet to rebuild. The CRM must stay up to date: next payment date, cash or PIK interest, recoverability.
Sacrifice
MEDIUM. Analysts give up their own order of work for a shared rule. One more screen next to the CRM.

HEAD OF CREDIT

Outcome
"No borrower defaults without us seeing it coming, and the team works on the most money at risk each day."
Likelihood
LOW-MEDIUM. Depends on how many borrowers' bank data is connected, how complete the CRM is, and a threshold set on few known missed payments.
Delay
HIGH. Missed payments are rare, so recall takes quarters to measure.
Effort
HIGH. Bank and CRM connectors, data quality checks, access control and a data protection review.
Sacrifice
MEDIUM. Borrower bank data sits in one more system. Catching every miss first means accepting more false alarms.

Verdict: likelihood is weakest for both.

  • Analyst fix: test the forecast on past data. Each past snapshot gets its answer once its payment date passes. If actual cash on the payment date (T1) lands above P10 about 90% of the time, the forecast is well set. This costs data, not users.
  • Head of credit fix: sit with two analysts. Compare their order of work with the tool's list on the same day. If the orders differ, ask which loans they would have handled first and why. This tests the ranking rule before any build.
Sub-problems
  1. Trapped cash. The cash total includes currencies the borrower can't convert or send, so coverage looks fine when it isn't.
  2. Future cash. Cash on the payment date is unknown today, and a single guess hides the bad case.
  3. Order of work. Each analyst handles borrowers in their own order, not by money at stake x recoverability.
  4. Trust. A warning nobody can check gets rebuilt in a spreadsheet or ignored.
  5. Follow-through. Actions and outcomes aren't recorded, so there's no history to set the threshold.
  6. Data plumbing. Bank balances and loan terms sit in separate systems, updated at different times.
  7. Recoverability. Whether a borrower can fix a shortfall, and what the lender gets back if not, needs financial statements, the lender's place in the repayment queue and the loan's conditions.
  8. Calibration. Setting the threshold needs past missed payments to learn from, and few exist.

This build tackles 1 to 5.

How it works
  1. Input. Simulated bank data (account, currency, balance in local currency, exchange rate, and how convertible the currency is) and a simulated CRM loan record (principal, interest rate, payments per year, share paid in cash, next payment date, recoverability).
  2. Usable cash. Local balance x exchange rate x a factor: 1 if freely convertible, 0.5 if partly convertible (assumed discount), 0 if blocked.
  3. Range on the payment date (T1). P50 = usable cash minus daily spending x days to T1. Spread = the usual daily swing in cash (standard deviation) x square root of days. P40 and P10 sit 0.25 and 1.28 spreads below P50.
  4. Cash due. Principal x interest rate / payments per year x cash share. A loan with all interest added to the loan (full PIK) owes no cash at T1.
  5. Coverage and band. Coverage = P50 / cash due. Band = P10 / due to P40 / due. This is the liquidity-at-risk method from the assignment deck: how low usable cash could fall in a bad case.
  6. Level. Cash due at or below P10: None. Up to P40: Low. Up to P50: Medium. Above P50: High. If bank data is more than 3 days old, the level is raised to at least Low.
  7. Rank and priority. Worst level first, then principal at risk, then coverage. High and Medium get P1 to P4, split by principal of $2M or more and recoverability score of 0.5 or more.
  8. Output. A ranked list, a trace of every number to its source field, and an action log.
Hypotheses, one per tackled sub-problem

1 · TRAPPED CASH

Before -> after
coverage on all cash -> coverage on usable cash only
Hypothesis
If coverage counts only cash the borrower can send, then fewer borrowers look safe just before a missed payment, because trapped cash can't pay interest owed in dollars, euros or pounds.
Metric
Share of missed payments where coverage was 1.0 or more one period before. Down. Baseline unobserved. Guardrail: false alarm rate (warnings where the borrower then paid in full) stays under the limit the head of credit sets.
Test
On past data with known outcomes, compute coverage on all cash and on usable cash. Count the misses each one flagged.

2 · FUTURE CASH

Before -> after
today's cash vs the payment -> P10, P40, P50 range on the payment date
Hypothesis
If the warning depends on where cash due falls in the range, then more misses are flagged in time at the same false alarm rate, because the threshold sits at the bad-case end, not the middle.
Metric
Recall: share of missed payments flagged at least N days before T1 (N set by the credit team). Up. Baseline unobserved.
Test
Test on past snapshots kept aside. Check the range (actual cash above P10 about 90%, above P40 about 60%), then recall vs precision at each cut-off.

3 · ORDER OF WORK

Before -> after
each analyst's own order -> worst first, then P1 to P4 by money x recoverability
Hypothesis
If the list is sorted by level and principal at risk, then more principal at risk gets an action on the day of the warning, because the largest loans that can still be saved are handled first.
Metric
Principal at risk with an action logged within one day of the warning. Up. Baseline unobserved.
Test
Watch two analysts sort their day's work, then run the same day's list in the tool. Compare order and principal covered in the first hour.

4 · TRUST

Before -> after
numbers rebuilt in a spreadsheet -> each number traced to its CRM or bank field
Hypothesis
If every number shows its formula and source field, then analysts act on warnings sooner, because checking takes a click, not a rebuild.
Metric
Time from warning to first action. Down. Baseline unobserved. Also: share of warnings dismissed as wrong.
Test
Five flagged cases. Time to check with raw files only vs with the trace.

5 · FOLLOW-THROUGH

Before -> after
actions in email and memory -> logged with level and coverage at the time
Hypothesis
If logging sits on the same screen as the warning, then more warnings end in a recorded action, because the step costs one click. Each record also becomes data for setting the threshold.
Metric
Share of High and Medium warnings with an action logged within the agreed time (deck: e.g. one day). Up. Baseline unobserved.
Test
Count over a four-week pilot.

Observed in the build
  • Sample bank file: 62 accounts, 22 currencies, $9,311,675 total. Only $795,714 (8.5%) sits in US dollars, euros or pounds (USD, EUR or GBP). This supports hypothesis 1. The deck said 82% was trapped; the file shows 91%.
  • Missing IDs: 17 of 62 rows have no account ID and hold $2,573,737 (28%). Without a way to build an ID, that cash drops out of the count.
  • Tests: demo/test.js runs 19 checks on 3 fixed borrowers plus 2 bank data events. 19 pass.
  • Demo event: a euro freeze at Agri A moves coverage from 1.04 to 0.38, Low to High, rank 3 to 1. This shows how the logic of hypotheses 1 and 2 works, not whether it's accurate.
  • Recall-first (warn early, accept more false alarms): Telecom C has coverage 1.02 but level Medium, because cash due sits above P40. The warning fires before coverage drops below 1.
  • Users: nothing observed. No analyst has used either the prototype or the demo.
Limits and trade-offs
  • Data: the borrowers are made up. The sample bank file is one snapshot in time, so spending rate and months of cash left can't be computed from it.
  • Users: untested. Every "before" is assumed.
  • Accuracy: unmeasured. Hypothesis 2 depends on an assumption: cash falls by the same amount each day, with bell-curve swings.
  • Discounts: counting partly convertible cash at 50% is assumed. The currency categories need checking with the team and the currency (FX) desks.
  • Money at stake: principal at risk is the full principal, not an expected loss.
  • Demo: one loan per borrower, fixed exchange rates, and everything resets on reload.
