Shield

Finds events in a construction project's letters and reports that could cost the owners money. Shows each contract deadline and drafts the formal notice for a named person to approve.

Infrastructure investingOverseeing fund investmentsPrototypeFictional data

Problem

When a contractor claims extra money, or a contract notice deadline is missed, money moves from the project company to the contractor. The fund that owns part of the project often first sees the loss when its stake is revalued at quarter end.

Example: in the demo, the project company has 6 days left to send notice about rock found on site. The fund's share of what's at stake is $6.63M.

For

OrganisationAn infrastructure fund that owns part of a project company building something large, such as a gas plant
UserThe fund's asset manager (the person overseeing this investment). Decides which events to raise with the project company, and whether a draft notice can go out
BuyerA fund partner or the head of asset management. Decides whether to pay for the tool across all the fund's projects, and what goes into each quarter's valuation of the stake

Terms on this page

  • Infrastructure fund: a fund that invests in roads, power plants and similar projects.
  • Project company (ProjectCo): the company that owns the project and signs the building contract.
  • Contractor (EPC): the firm hired to design, buy for and build the project.
  • Claim: a formal request for extra money or time under the contract.
  • Notice deadline (time bar): the last day to send a written notice. Miss it and the right to claim can be lost.
  • Clause, amendment: a numbered rule in the contract, and a later change to it.
  • Contingency: money set aside in the budget for overruns.
  • Fund equity at risk: the part of a loss that comes out of the fund's own money.

User stories

  1. Deadline. As an asset manager, I want every open notice deadline listed with the documents behind it, so the project company sends notice before the deadline passes.
  2. Equity at risk. As an asset manager, I want each event's cost shown as the fund's share, so I can tell the partner before the quarterly valuation.
  3. Control. As a fund partner, I want every notice approved by a named person and logged, so the fund can show who decided what.

Try it

  1. Pick an event the tool found. EV-1 has 6 days left before the project company's notice deadline.
  2. Follow the document trail: letter, site record, schedule entry, contract clause, deadline countdown. Below it, read how much of the fund's own money is at risk.
  3. Read the draft notice, then approve or reject it. The decision goes into the log. Switch "Acting as" to the analyst to see the tool refuse someone who isn't allowed to approve.

Sample data only. The fund, project company, project and contractor are fictional. "Today" is fixed at 26 Aug 2026. The page runs in the browser, with no server and no AI model call. The agent's outputs were computed beforehand and saved into the page.

Details

Value equation

value = (outcome x likelihood) / (time delay x effort & sacrifice)

ASSET MANAGER

Outcome
"I hear about a claim or a notice deadline weeks before the quarter ends, with the documents, and the project company sends notice on time."
Likelihood
MEDIUM. Every deadline and quote can be checked. The tool found 3 of 4 events in the test set. No asset manager has tried it.
Delay
LOW. It reads documents the project company already holds. Time to first alert is unmeasured.
Effort
MEDIUM. Needs read access to the project company's document and schedule files. Each draft needs a human review.
Sacrifice
MEDIUM. The project company's managers may see it as the fund checking their work. A list of known risks can be handed to the other side in a legal dispute.

FUND PARTNER

Outcome
"No drop in our stake's value from a claim we could have seen. Each cut traces back to a document."
Likelihood
LOW. That earlier notices lead to fewer surprise drops in value is a hypothesis. There's no data across the fund's projects.
Delay
HIGH. The effect on valuations shows over several quarters.
Effort
MEDIUM. Document access depends on the fund's right to information in each project.
Sacrifice
MEDIUM. Some goodwill with the project company's managers. Lawyers must review the list of risks.

Verdict: the outcome is clear for both. The partner's likelihood and delay are weakest.

  • Fix 1, backtest: run it on the archive of one finished project. Count the drops in stake value caused by claims and similar events. Check whether the documents showed each one earlier. This gives evidence in weeks, not quarters.
  • Fix 2, sacrifice: run it read-only, with the project company's lawyers owning the list of risks. The risk of handing it over in a dispute then sits with the party that already holds the documents.
Sub-problems

"Commercial event" here means something in a document, like a late site handover, that can start a notice deadline or a claim.

  1. Detection. Signs of a claim sit in letters, meeting minutes and daily site reports that nobody reads in full.
  2. Evidence. Linking a letter to its site record, schedule entry and clause is done by hand.
  3. Deadline. Each notice deadline depends on the clause and its amendments. Missing one can cost the right to claim.
  4. Equity view. Event costs are stated for the whole project. The fund thinks in terms of its own share.
  5. Control. A draft notice must not go out without a named person's approval.
  6. Claim value. Working out what a claim is worth needs a construction cost expert.
  7. Reporting. Events need to reach the quarterly valuation and the report to the fund's own investors.
  8. All projects. Each project has its own contract form, amendments and document systems.

This build tackles 1 to 5. 1, 2 and 4 are partial.

How it works
  1. Read in: documents arrive. Documents from another project are refused and logged.
  2. Find: each document is scanned for trigger phrases. Each hit gets a clause and a confidence score. Below 0.6, it stays hidden.
  3. Check: each quote must appear word for word in its source document, or the event is marked down.
  4. Deadline: the event date plus the clause's notice period. If an amendment is missing, the date is marked provisional.
  5. Value (demo): days of delay beyond the schedule's spare days (float), times the cost per day, plus direct costs. The part above the remaining contingency, times the fund's share, is fund equity at risk.
  6. Draft: a notice that cites each source, with status PENDING_APPROVAL.
  7. Approval: only a person on the approver list can approve. Every approval, rejection and refusal is logged. Nothing is sent.
Hypotheses, one per tackled sub-problem

1 · DETECTION

Before -> after
event found when the formal claim arrives or in the quarterly report -> flagged on the date of the document that shows it
Hypothesis
If every letter, minute and daily report is checked against the contract's trigger phrases, then the asset manager learns of an event sooner, because the signs are in the documents before the formal claim.
Metric
Days from the first document to the asset manager knowing. Down. Baseline unobserved. Guardrail: precision (share of alerts that are real) at or above 0.85 (test: 1.00 on 8 documents).
Test
Run it on one finished project's archive and compare with a list of events marked by an expert.

2 · EVIDENCE

Before -> after
trail put together by hand -> each link's quote checked against its source
Hypothesis
If each event arrives with checked links from letter to clause, then review time per event goes down, because the reviewer checks links instead of searching for them.
Metric
Minutes to review one event; share of trails needing correction. Down. Baseline unobserved.
Test
Two reviewers, 10 events, with and without the trail.

3 · DEADLINE

Before -> after
periods tracked from memory or a spreadsheet -> computed from the clause and its amendments, provisional if one is missing
Hypothesis
If deadlines come from the clause and all its amendments, then fewer deadlines pass without a notice, because errors like 28 days vs an amended 21 are caught.
Metric
Deadlines passed without a notice, per project per year. Down. Baseline unobserved.
Test
Recompute deadlines on a finished project and compare with the notices actually sent.

4 · EQUITY VIEW

Before -> after
whole-project dollars, seen in the quarterly report -> fund's own money at risk per event, inputs on the page
Hypothesis
If the asset manager sees fund equity at risk when an event is found, then fewer drops in value reach the partner as a surprise, because the partner hears before the valuation date.
Metric
Share of event-driven drops in value flagged before quarter end. Up. Baseline unobserved.
Test
Go through the last 4 quarters of one project. For each write-down, was there an earlier document trail?

5 · CONTROL

Before -> after
drafts by email, approver unclear -> approver list, logged decision, no send function
Hypothesis
If only people on the approver list can approve and every decision is logged, then no notice leaves without an owner, because the system can't send and refuses anyone else.
Metric
Notices sent without a logged approver. Down to zero. Test: 3 of 3 notices stopped at PENDING_APPROVAL.
Test
Approval rule tests, plus attempts to approve as someone not on the list.

Observed in the build
  • Test run (2026-10-01, 8 fictional documents): 17 of 17 tests passed. Precision 1.00 (3 of 3 alerts real), recall 0.75 (3 of 4 events found, missed LTR-006), 2 of 2 non-events correctly ignored. Supports 1, with a gap in recall.
  • Evidence: 3 of 3 shown citations verified. A test setup built to invent citations produced 7 fake ones; 0 were shown. Supports 2.
  • Deadlines: 3 of 3 match the expected dates, incl. 1 flagged provisional for a missing amendment. Supports 3.
  • Approval controls: 1 document from another project refused. 3 of 3 notices stopped at PENDING_APPROVAL. A person not on the approver list was refused. 21 audit log entries, tamper check (hash chain) intact. Supports 5.
  • Demo tests: 10 of 10 pass (node --test demo/tests/calc.test.js). They cover deadlines, amendments, status cut-offs, matching the agent's 3 deadlines, 15 trail quotes and value at risk.
  • Demo figures: EV-1 shows $19.58M at stake for the project and $6.63M of fund equity at risk. All 3 events total $9.34M, 3.9% of the stake's value. Inputs are fictional, so these show how the calculation works, not a result. Relates to 4.
Limits and trade-offs
  • Data: fictional. 8 test documents. The demo shows 15 documents, 2 of them added for the demo.
  • Users: no asset manager or partner has used it. Every "before" is assumed.
  • Detection: keyword rules, not an AI model. Accuracy on real letters is unmeasured.
  • Awareness date: the agent uses the document date. Contracts often start the clock when a party "became aware, or should have", which can be earlier.
  • Value: assumes every open dispute is lost, and that the owners' money covers overruns above contingency. That overstates the expected loss.
  • Who approves: the demo makes the asset manager the approver. In practice the project company sends notices, and the fund acts through its rights as a shareholder.
  • Source mismatch: EV-1 in the demo uses the walkthrough record (deadline amended to 21 days). The agent's test version of the same event uses 28 days.
